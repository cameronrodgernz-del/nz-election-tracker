How the tracker works

  1. Every poll goes in

    All published party-vote polls since the 2023 election: 1News-Verian, RNZ-Reid Research, Taxpayers' Union-Curia, Roy Morgan, The Post-Freshwater and Anacta (Talbot Mills until it was renamed in September). A poll is placed at the middle of its fieldwork, and bigger samples count for a little more.

  2. Each pollster's lean comes out

    Every pollster runs a little high or low for some parties. The tracker measures each one against the others over the past 16 months, with recent polls counting more, and takes it out. The middle is set mainly by 1News-Verian, RNZ-Reid Research and Curia, with the others counting for a little less, so no single pollster decides where the line sits.

  3. One line per party

    Each party's support is treated as something that drifts from day to day. The tracker draws the line that best fits every poll at once, and how fast a party's support has really been moving decides how hard one new poll can pull the line. The band shows how sure it is.

  4. Allowing for the polls being wrong together

    In 2017, 2020 and 2023 the final polls as a group missed some parties by a few points, and the Greens tended to poll above their result. The election-day estimates build in misses of that size, shared by every pollster, plus a small correction for the Greens. A miss for National tends to come with the opposite miss for Labour.

  5. Running the election

    The tracker then runs the election — times. Each run draws a party vote for every party, settles the electorates, applies the — threshold (a party that wins an electorate is in regardless), shares out the seats with the Sainte-Laguë formula the Electoral Commission uses, and keeps any overhang seats.

  6. Electorates

    Each general electorate starts from who would have won it in 2023 on the new boundaries drawn in 2025, using The Tally Room's estimates where they are published and the tracker's own estimates for the two new Wellington seats, Kapiti and Kenepuru. A party whose MP is standing down loses about two points, and National MPs first elected in 2023 get a smaller lift. Each seat then moves with the national swing between National and Labour, with room for local surprises. Where a local poll has been published (Hutt South, Kapiti, Waitaki, West Coast-Tasman, Mount Albert, Auckland Central) it is blended in. Auckland Central is treated as National against the Greens, and Tāmaki as National against ACT. The Māori electorates follow the Whakaata Māori–Curia seat polls and the news since.

  7. Who can govern

    National, ACT and NZ First are counted as one side; Labour, the Greens and Te Pāti Māori as the other. Luxon and Peters have both ruled out working with Opportunity, so on stated positions Opportunity can only help Hipkins. The second view relaxes that: Opportunity talks first to whichever of National and Labour has more seats. The third view is the first with National's extra electorate seats taken away, to show how much rides on them.

  8. What it cannot see

    A late swing after the last polls, a big change in who turns out, and coalition talks that break from what the leaders have said. The electorate part is the simplest piece: most seats have no poll, so they rest on the 2023 result and the national swing, and that drives how many extra seats National is likely to win.
